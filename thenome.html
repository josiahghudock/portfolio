About The 'Nome

The 'Nome is a free user-friendly online metronome with advanced capabilities. It is built specifically for practicing odd-time, polyrhythmic, odd-tuplet, or nested tuplet rhythms. Ultra-Nerd mode allows for "irrational" time signatures and extremely precise sequencing. If you found this tool useful, have questions, or suggestions please let me know!

The 'Nome: user guide

What is The 'Nome?

The 'Nome is a tool musicians can use to work on their timing. The metronome keeps perfect time. Musicians can compare their timing to the machine's or use it as a foundation for the construction of their own rhythms.

Is it Just Another Metronome?

No! The 'Nome takes many features available from different apps and combines it into one free user-friendly tool. Metronomes can be built for different purposes. The 'Nome takes a more mathematical approach to rhythm. It allows for precise timing controls for advanced musicians, including features for polyrhythms, nested tuplets, and irrational time signatures. In this regard, The 'Nome is unmatched in its technicality.

A traditional metronome sounds at every beat, but a beat sequencer like The 'Nome allows musicians to construct custom rhythmic sequences to use as a metronomic reference.

Using The 'Nome:

The Basics:

First, set your favorite BPM tempo. Hitting play, The 'Nome acts as a traditional metronome would, counting off bars of 4 at the chosen tempo.

To customize your metronome, you can toggle certain beats on or off by clicking on their spot on the circle. You can also adjust the number of beats, allowing for all X/4 standard time signatures.

If you want to adjust the pitch or timbre of the sound, you can do so in Sound Settings & Synthesis. You can also adjust the volume of each individual beat, for instance if you wanted an accent on the first beat.

Pro Tip If you ever get lost, the Reset 4/4 button will bring you right back to the beginning.

Polyrhythm:

Colloquially, a polyrhythm occurs when two different rhythms are played against each other. Strictly speaking, a x:y polyrhythm occurs when x beats are played against y beats, all equally spaced, and such that the two rhythms align on the first beat. The most popular polyrhythm is 3:2, the so-called "hemiola". The 'Nome allows for both polyrhythmic and polymetric constructions.

By activating polyrhythm and selecting a beat count, you can instantly superimpose any number of beats over your existing beat sequence.

The 'Nome: metric rhythmic theory

Metric Rhythms:

Rhythm is just the temporal dimension of music. It asks when notes are heard and when they are silent. There doesn't have to be any rhyme or reason to what happens when. Free flowing rhythms which are left up to the whims of the performers are called non-metric rhythms. Alternatively, metric rhythms are entirely based around an underlying mathematical structure. This doesn't mean they are boring and rigid however. At least they don't have to be. Check out this example of a loose, flowy, and disorienting yet perfectly metric rhythm:

You don't have to do any math if you don't want to. However, fluency in the following topics allows for a great range of rhythmic diversity and tools for rhythmic development which facilitate groove, coordination between performers, and add a sophisticated aesthetic flair to a composition.

Tempo and Pulse

Pulse

The pulse is the backbone of many rhythmic constructions. It is a (sometimes inaudible) string of equally spaced beats at a given speed. The pulse serves several important functions in metric rhythms: It often contains the strongest notes in a phrase, the space between its beats can be subdivided into any number of beats, polyrhythms can be superimposed on its structure, and for many (but not all) rhythms it is what the performer will "hear" via audiation.

We can view time as a line, and the pulse selects a uniform sequence of notes along the line. To construct our rhythm, at each beat we choose either to play or to rest. Cutting a line at its start/end and glueing its ends together gives the circle notation used here.

Tempo

The tempo is the speed at which the beats occur. The faster the tempo, the shorter the distance between two beats. The rate of change is usually measured in beats per minute (BPM).

Terminology:

Traditionally, the quarter note is typically the "beat" that is referred to by BPM. A quarter note is called "quarter" because it splits a measure into 4ths, when the time signature is 4/4. Because of this, the word "quarter" makes considerably less sense when working with alternative time signatures. We will simply refer to them as beats, with the exception of the beat-click pulse rate which conforms with this quarter note convention.

The useful range of tempo is biologically constrained to be around 30-300 BPM, below which we can no longer predict the next beat and above which we can not recognize distinct beats. Read More

Subdivisions

If we think of the pulse as points spread out on a line (or circle), there is a lot of empty space between beats where we might want the onset of a note to lie, especially with slower tempos. Subdivisions allow us to fill in these gaps.

In a subdivision, we split the space between a beat into equally spaced notes, beginning at the start of the beat.

Terminology:

Tuplets describe the specific number of beats in a subdivision. Traditionally, we have whole, half, quarter, eighth, and sixteenth notes as the dyadic subdivisions. Triplets then need to be specified, whether we are considering 3 notes within the duration of a quarter, an eighth, etc.. So we have quarter note triplets and eighth note triplets as the way to differentiate between the two subdivisions. This makes considerably less sense when tuplets are compared against any other subdivision type, so we dispose of this terminology in favor of a more general notion.

We will call a general subdivision to be an x-let where x is the number of notes in the subdivision. A beat itself is a 1-let, split in half gives us a 2-let, into thirds gives us a 3-let, and so on. So traditional sixteenth notes are 4-lets, and traditional eight-note triplets are 3-lets.

So one way we can access the space in between two beats by first fixing a subdivision, and then selecting which notes within that subdivision to play/rest.

This still leaves a large area between beats unreachable by any single subdivision. To access these more foreign areas, we need nested tuplets, i.e. recursive subdivision.

Terminology:

A nested tuplet is a tuplet within a tuplet. We can take one subdivision of the beat to be the first tuplet. Then we can split the space between any two notes in that subdivision further into some number of notes for a second layer of subdivision.

Note that there are multiple ways to write identical rhythms. For example, we can write a 4-let as 2-lets nested inside 2-lets:

Any composite numbered tuplet can be written as a nested tuplet, while primes are distinct.

In metric rhythm, anywhere in time we want to play a note must be accessible via some short sequence of rhythmic operations: tuplets, nested tuplets, and polyrhythm. Theoretically, and for electronic compositions, there is no limit to the places we can reach with these methods. In practice, there's an upper bound to the speed of a subdivision that we can keep track of, or the precision we may achieve.

One useful trick for dealing with large tuplets is to chunk them into longs and shorts. For instance, a quick 7-let can be thought of as 2+2+2+1, or long long long short.

Polyrhythm

A polyrhythm occurs when two or more strings of evenly spaced notes are played at the same time such that they start and end together. They are denoted by the number of notes in each string. For example, a 2-let played against a 3-let yields a 2 against 3 polyrthym, or 2:3.

Polyrhythms can occur both at the level of beats and at the level of subdivision. Polyrthyms at the level of beats can be written in terms of uniform subdivision by a process of grouping. For example, we can construct a 7 against 3 polyrhythm in the following way: take 3 beats from the pulse as the 3-part, and subdivide them each into 7-lets, playing every third note to get the 7-part:

This process of splitting subdivision into evenly sized groups and playing the first note allows for the calculation of various polyrhythms.

Polyrhythmic subdivision:

Polyrhythms can also be performed on the level of subdivisions. For example, we can perform a 5 against 3 polyrhythm by first splitting a beat into 3-lets, and choosing those to be the 3-part, then playing 5 evenly spaced notes against those to be the 5-part. We could also split two beats into 3-lets, and take the third note of the first beat, and the first two notes of the second beat to be the 3-part, thus extending our polyrhythms "over the beat" or "over the bar". These types of polyrhythms are used in . With these capabilities, the possibilities are vast.

In general, we can think of an x against y polyrhythm as x evenly spaced notes squeezed within the duration of y notes. The y notes can be fixed at a given tuplet rate r, I.e. y beats corresponds to r=1, y 3-lets corresponds to r=3 etc..

Time Signature

A time signature is an additional layer of organization on a rhythm. It determines the number of beats in a bar, where a bar can be considered some chunk of the rhythm. Most popular music is played in time signatures of 4 or 3, but we can have time signatures of 5 beats, 7 beats, or whatever we want!

Mathematically, the time signature does not alter the rhythm itself, only how it is displayed to the performer. Historically, there are conventional differences between mathematically equivalent time signatures such as 4/4 and 6/8. However, the same rhythm can be represented by infinitely many different time signatures. Because The 'Nome is not a standard notation system, we do not place much emphasis on time signatures here.

Non-Dyadic Time Signatures:

Traditionally, a time signature is represented as two numbers, where the first tells you how many beats are in a measure, and the second tells you what type of note is counted as a beat. In regards to the second number, a 4 represents a quarter note, an 8 represents an eigth note, and so on. The powers of 2 give us what are called the dyadic time signatures.

In theory, there is nothing stopping us from considering a measure to consist of 3 quintuplet notes, but if you hand any working musician a piece composed using 3/5 time, they will more likely than not be utterly confused.

However, these non-dyadic times are useful conceptually. if for instance, we want a phrase to be played at the rate of a 5-let, but we have an 8 note melody to play, we may write this as a bar of 8/5 times. This gives the rhythmic effect of "skipping a beat", even more so than your typical odd-time signature. The 7/4 time feels like it is skipping a beat, but the 8/5 time feels like it is skipping two fifths of a beat.
